# Dono — Pitch Script & Playbook

Companion to **`Dono_Investor_Pitch.pptx`** (20 slides, built for a 10-minute pitch).

---

## 1. The one-sentence investment thesis

> **Universities have industrialised the £10,000 gift and abandoned the £400 one — and in doing so they have written off an entire generation of donors. Dono is the rail that gets that generation their first gift, and owns the relationship afterwards.**

Everything in the deck serves that sentence. If a slide doesn't advance it, it isn't in the deck.

**Why it's venture-scale (say this in Q&A, not on a slide):** the fee is the floor. What compounds is the only cross-institution graph of verified young donors in UK higher education — which no college can build, because they are siloed from one another by GDPR and by internal competition for the same alumni. Every college is a walled garden. Dono is the only party standing outside all of them, because the donor opts in to *us*.

## 2. The three narrative strands

You asked for all three planned out. Here they are, with the actual words. **The deck as built runs Strand C.** Strands A and B are drop-in replacements for slides 1–3 only — everything from slide 4 onwards is unchanged.

### Strand A — "Students can't raise money"

**Spine:** the system fails the person asking.
**Opens on:** a rejection.
**Emotional register:** injustice.

**Opening script (≈45s):**
> "£400. [pause] That was a flight to Estonia — to represent Oxford at the International Anatomy Olympiad. I applied to my college for it. I was turned down.
>
> I'm not unusual. We surveyed twenty-three Oxford students. Of everyone who went through the formal route — college grant, hardship fund, travel fund — seventy-three per cent were turned down or given less than they asked for.
>
> And the ones who didn't apply mostly didn't apply because they were too embarrassed to ask. Fourteen out of twenty-three.
>
> So here's what actually happens. The opportunity doesn't get taken, or it gets paid for out of a student loan. Quietly. Every year."

**Strengths:** most emotionally direct. Best possible answer to "does the team care?" (innovation/passion criterion). The £400 story is unfakeable.
**Risks:** frames Dono as a hardship charity — dangerous, because your own survey shows the deepest-hardship respondent was the *least* likely to post. Also implicitly criticises colleges, which Roger specifically warned against, and makes the alumni (your actual paying customer) an afterthought.
**Use if:** the judging panel skews toward social impact rather than commercial return.

### Strand B — "Alumni need transparency"

**Spine:** the system fails the person giving.
**Opens on:** a market, not a person.
**Emotional register:** indignation at waste.

**Opening script (≈45s):**
> "Last year the British public gave fourteen billion pounds to charity. The year before, it was fifteen point four. One point four billion pounds walked out of the sector in twelve months.
>
> Everyone assumes people stopped caring. They didn't. Ask them why, and four in ten say the same thing: they don't believe their money reached the cause.
>
> [pause] That's not apathy. That's a receipts problem.
>
> People don't dislike giving. They dislike giving into a void — and every institution in this country is still asking them to."

**Strengths:** Roger's gut-feel pick, and he's right that it's the least adversarial toward development offices. Puts your paying customer at the centre from second one. Naturally sets up "trust requires proof", which is your defensibility. Scales beyond universities — this framing lets you claim the whole £14bn market rather than a student niche.
**Risks:** starts with a statistic, not a human. Cold. Judges scoring "originality and passion" get nothing to hold onto for the first minute, and you burn your single best asset — the personal story.
**Use if:** you're pitching to a purely commercial panel, or if a co-presenter without a personal funding story is opening.

### Strand C — Braided *(recommended, and what the deck runs)*

**Spine:** both people are stuck, and they're stuck *on each other*. One missing piece of infrastructure, two victims, one market.
**Opens on:** the person. **Pivots within 40 seconds** to the market.
**Emotional register:** injustice → recognition.

The pivot is the whole trick, and it lives on **slide 3**:

> "That college has nine thousand living alumni. Its development office deliberately talks to two hundred of them. That's not a criticism — it's rational, and it's how every development office in this country is run.
>
> But it leaves eight thousand eight hundred people. Eight thousand eight hundred people who rowed in that boat club, sat that exam, loved that place — and who would have given me ten pounds without blinking.
>
> There was no way on earth to reach them."

**Why this wins:** it converts a personal anecdote into a market-structure argument in one move, and it does it with *their* number, not yours. The judge stops hearing "a student wants money" and starts hearing "there is a supply of capital with no distribution channel." That is an investment thesis, and it arrives ninety seconds in.

It also lets you have Roger's shock-and-awe *and* his non-adversarial alumni framing, instead of choosing.

**Swap instructions:** to run A or B instead, replace slides 1–3 with the scripts above and change slide 4's sub-line from "The giving platform for the 8,800" to "Where every pound is accounted for" (Strand B) or "Nobody should have to pay for their own opportunity" (Strand A).

### Slide 18 — The ask **(FIGURES BLANK — must be filled)**
**Objective:** win the tie-break point. The rubric awards 5 points for whether the use of funds is clear and practically impactful.
**Content:** headline figure and milestone (blank), four use-of-funds cards with blank amounts — acquisition · legal & incorporation · build-out · runway — plus "what it buys you".

**How to derive the number (do this before Tuesday):**
1. Pick the 18-month milestone that makes the next raise trivial. Suggest: *120 active campaigns across 8–10 colleges, and 30% of donors giving a second time.*

3. Cost each: ambassador stipends × colleges; paid acquisition per activated society; solicitor sign-off (get a real quote); incorporation + IP assignment + trademark; build-out months.

5. Sanity-check backwards: at 3.5% of GMV, what revenue does the milestone generate? If it's a small fraction of the ask, say so before a judge does — and explain that you're buying the *graph*, not the fee income, in this phase.

## 7. Q&A preparation — the eight questions you will get

**1. "Aren't you competing with the development offices?"**
No, and it's the first thing we made sure of. They pay us nothing, they chase gifts above £10,000, they deliberately don't ask under-thirties, and they fund buildings not lab coats. Four of the eleven offices we interviewed independently told us small donors become big donors. We manufacture their pipeline for free.

**2. "Why would a donor volunteer to pay a fee?"**
Because they already do, in this exact market. Two Oxford colleges run donor-covers-the-fee checkouts today; one saves about £3,000 a year because donors opt in. We also tested the alternative — students told us a platform taking a cut of *their* money would put them off using it.

**3. "£150 campaigns at 3.5% is £5. How is that a business?"**
It isn't, at one campaign. The unit that matters is the donor, not the campaign — a donor who gives four times a year across three campaigns. And in this phase we aren't optimising fee income; we're building the only cross-institution record of verified young donors in UK higher education, which no single college can legally assemble.

**4. "Oxford already tried this and it failed."**
OxReach. Six projects, four from professors, not renewed. It failed because it was built for the institution and sold to the institution. We're free to the institution and built for the student and the donor. That's not a small difference — it's the whole difference.

**5. "How do you stop fraud?"**
Three layers. Verification: university email plus student card. Human review of every campaign before publication. And structurally — money never touches us. It's a direct Stripe charge into the campaign's own connected account, which means Stripe carries KYC and dispute liability, and we cannot hold, delay or divert anyone's money.

**6. "What if a campaign doesn't spend the money on what it promised?"**
Every campaign posts purchase evidence and a closure statement. Under-spend is refunded, most recent donor first. And a campaign cannot be funded beyond its target, so over-funding doesn't arise.

**7. "Why hasn't a big platform done this?"**
Because the verification and the community layer are the product, and neither is worth building for a £15 gift unless you're native to the institution. GoFundMe can process the payment. It can't tell you whether the person asking is really a second-year at Somerville, and it has no reason to keep you connected to Somerville after the campaign closes.

**8. "What's the biggest risk?"**
Getting the first college over the line. Colleges copy colleges — one told us plainly they'll join once they see it working elsewhere — so the first one is the only genuinely hard one, and it's what most of the funding is for. *(Answer this one honestly and specifically. Judges score candour.)*

## 8. Delivery notes

- **Rehearse the first ninety seconds until it's muscle memory.** Slides 1–3 decide how the rest of the pitch is heard.
- **Pauses are the technique.** Three real pauses: after "£400", after "I was turned down", after "8,800 people".
- **Never apologise for stage of company.** "We have raised nothing" is a statement of fact delivered flat, not a confession.
- **Q&A chemistry beats Q&A knowledge.** Hand questions to the right person by name ("Joe owns the payments architecture — Joe?"). Never interrupt each other. Never contradict a teammate in front of the panel; if you disagree, say "we've argued about this — Amrit's view is X, mine is Y" and let it stand. Panels read that as a functioning team, not a divided one.
- **The panel is friendly but informed.** Assume at least one person in the room has run a fundraising operation and at least one is a lawyer. The payments-architecture line on slide 10 and the anonymisation of institutions are both for them.

## 10. Things I changed, and things you should check

**Institutions are anonymised throughout.** One Head of Development explicitly asked that her college not be named in any written material. Rather than anonymise one and name the rest, the deck attributes everything by role and scale ("Director of Development, 10,000-alumni Oxford college"). This is safer reputationally, keeps every one of those relationships intact, and reads as *more* credible, not less. If you want to name a college, get that person's written consent first.

**Quotes are compressed paraphrases from your meeting notes, not verbatim transcripts.** They're all ≤8 words per Roger's instruction and all faithful to the notes — but if a judge asks "is that a direct quote?", the honest answer is "that's how we recorded it in our notes." Don't present them as recorded speech.

**Three source documents contradict each other. The deck deliberately avoids all three claims:**

1. **Entity.** `TRUTH.md` (31 Jul, authoritative) says Dono is a trading name of a sole trader with no plan to incorporate. `corporate/founder-context-handoff.md` (16 Jul) describes five founders and a planned UK Ltd. The deck makes no entity claim and puts *incorporation* in the use-of-funds — which is true under either version. **Resolve this before pitching**, because "who owns it?" is a normal Q&A question.
2. **Insurance.** `company-context.md` says business insurance was added on 31 Jul; `TRUTH.md` of the same date says Dono is an uninsured sole trader. The deck claims neither. Worth reconciling in `TRUTH.md`.
3. **Team size.** Your pitch plan lists four founders; the corporate handoff describes five. The deck shows four. Add the fifth card if that's current — the slide has room.

**One claim I softened.** Your notes describe Community Funds as a product feature, but `TRUTH.md` prohibits pooled funds at launch. The deck doesn't mention them. Don't add them back for the pitch — a judge who asks about FCA permissions will find the inconsistency.

**Numbers used, and where they came from:** all Dono-primary except CAF *UK Giving 2026* (£15.4bn→£14bn) and UK government trust reporting (~6 in 10 believe donations reach the cause). The 73% figure is 8 of 11 survey respondents who applied for funding — small n, so if pressed, say "eight of the eleven students in our survey who applied" rather than defending a percentage.
