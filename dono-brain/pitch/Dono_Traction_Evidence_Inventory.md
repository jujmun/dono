# Dono — Traction & Validation Evidence Inventory

Everything in `dono-brain/` that could legitimately appear on a traction slide, graded by persuasive strength, with what to put on the slide and what to hold for Q&A.

**Sources mined:** student survey (26 responses / 23 current Oxford students, raw CSV re-tallied), 11 development-office and advisor interviews, company context, product overview, TRUTH.md.

## Part 1 — The framing problem, first

Most of what you have is **validation**, not **traction**. Investors and judges score those differently, and the fastest way to lose credibility is to present research as if it were revenue.

- **Traction** = things that happened. Users, campaigns, money moved, partners signed.
- **Validation** = evidence that they will happen. Interviews, surveys, letters of intent.

You are genuinely thin on the first and exceptionally strong on the second. So don't fight it — **lead with the strength and name it honestly**:

> "We have one live campaign. What we also have is the most thorough primary research anyone will put in front of you today — eleven development offices and twenty-six students, all our own."

That converts a weakness into the market-understanding criterion, which is worth 10 points and is the one you can win outright.

## Part 3 — TIER 2: Demand validation (the student side)

All re-tallied from the raw CSV. Denominators are the number who answered that question.

**The need is real and recurrent**
- **14 of 23** have needed money for a university activity
- Modal frequency **2–3 times in 12 months** (9 respondents); 3 more needed it 4–5 times
- Modal amount **£150–£500** (7 respondents), with a tail: 3 at £500–£1,000 and 3 at £1,000–£2,000
- **8 of 23** say their societies struggle to fund things *often or always*

**The existing system is failing them**
- **11 of 23** applied for a grant, hardship fund or travel fund
- **8 of those 11 were turned down or under-funded — 73%**

**The fallback is regressive, and sometimes there isn't one**
- 5 used their own savings, 4 used parents or family, 1 took extra paid work, 1 used their student loan
- **3 went without it entirely — forfeited the opportunity**

That last number is the emotional core of the whole dataset and it is currently unused. Three students in a sample of twenty-three simply did not do the thing.

**Intent, and the cross-tab that matters**
- Mean likelihood to create a campaign: **6.4 / 10**; **12 of 23 scored 7 or above**
- Mean usefulness of Dono to their time at university: **6.4 / 10**; 11 of 23 scored 7+
- **Students whose funding application was rejected score 7.1 — meaningfully above the 6.4 average**

That cross-tab is worth more than either headline number: **the people the current system failed are the most likely to use us.** That's product-market fit visible in the data, not asserted.

**What they'd actually use it for, with prices they named themselves**
Rowing blazer · £400 flights for research and work experience · Oxformals (£500) · MBAT (€700) · travel (~£700) · a research trip / internship abroad · learning resources and subscriptions (a few hundred) · a performing arts society campaign · conference and competition travel

**Trust is institutional, not product**
- **12 of 23** say the one thing that would make them trust Dono is *"my college or university officially endorsing it"* — more than friends using it (5), seeing real outcomes (3) and seeing where every pound goes (2) **combined**

**The barriers, honestly**
- **14** would feel embarrassed asking publicly
- **11** don't believe anyone would actually donate ← *your live campaign is the direct answer to this*
- 8 feel it's "not for me — others need it more"
- 7 cite effort; 7 cite privacy
- Only **4** flagged worry about fees, and only **1** named "taking fees" as a dealbreaker

That fee finding is important and counterintuitive — **fee resistance is a minority concern**, which supports the donor-pays model rather than undermining it. Don't hide it; use it.

**Visibility is a spectrum, not a wall**
- 10 are fine within their college or community, 8 are happy fully public, 2 want anonymity, 1 wants donor-only disclosure, **only 1 wouldn't post at all**
- So 21 of 22 would post at *some* visibility setting. The embarrassment problem is a design problem, not a demand problem — and you've already designed for it

**Unprompted feature demand**
- **Two respondents independently** asked for alumni matching for work experience / mentoring
- One asked for it to "be like Instagram" — unprompted validation of the community-and-feed model

## Part 4 — TIER 3: Institutional validation (professionals confirming your thesis)

These are the strongest items you own, because they are people paid to know this market describing your business back to you.

**The three that should be on the slide**

1. **A Director of Development at a 10,000-alumni college described Dono's exact opportunity, unprompted:** *"getting young alumni onto the donor ladder who wouldn't otherwise give, then nurturing them into broader college giving."*

2. **A graduate college's giving day raised £75,000 from 200 donors — against £56,000 from 200 donors five years earlier.** Donor count flat, average gift up ~34%. **This is the single best statistic in your entire research corpus and it is currently unused.** It proves institutions are not growing their donor base; they are extracting more from the same shrinking pool. That is the market failure in one line, and it came from a development office rather than from you.

3. **A Head of Development named your market boundary for you:** *"£2,000 is roughly the threshold where small-project fundraising becomes a problem for the development office."* Academics ask for £2,000 raises and the time cost is disproportionate.

**The rest, all usable**

- **"Small donors become big donors — you can't be dismissive of £5–£20 gifts."** Regular small gifts collectively keep the college running; large gifts are usually restricted and don't cover operating costs *(Worcester, ~9,100 alumni)*
- **"Small donations often function as test gifts"** — donors pilot a small gift to assess stewardship quality before committing more *(Hertford)*
- **"Even £10/month from a recent graduate builds a lifetime giving habit"** *(St Hugh's)*
- **"People like giving to people rather than institutions"** — human stories outperform facts and figures *(Magdalen)*
- **Colleges already fake the transparency you deliver.** Real restricted funds are administratively unviable at small amounts, so colleges use *"your £10 could fund X"* framing instead of actual restriction *(LMH)*. You do properly, at zero marginal cost, what they can only simulate
- **A college created a new discretionary fund ~2 years ago** specifically for things outside the JCR budget — subject dinners, furniture. **Institutional admission that the gap is real** *(Christ Church)*
- **Donor-covers-the-fee is already standard here.** One college's online portal lets donors cover bank fees and saves the college ~£3,000/year; another's donation provider does the same *(Univ, LMH)* — direct precedent for your revenue model
- **That same portal: 157 gifts this year, up 24% year on year** — the digital small-gift channel is the only one growing
- **A focused, single-narrative giving day outperformed previous multi-cause days** on both new donors and total raised *(Hertford)* — validates campaign specificity over general appeals
- **Oxfam research cited by a development office:** donors preferred honest reporting on challenges and delays over a rosy picture — validates radical transparency as a product decision
- **A giving-day donor message wall created a genuine sense of community** — *"I can see my friend's message here"* *(Hertford)* — validates your social layer
- **A college runs Community Bursaries: 10 awards a year at £5,000 each for entrepreneurial and extracurricular students, funded by a major donor** *(St Cross)* — proof that this exact category is fundable and that donors want to back it
- **Telethon economics are collapsing:** £30–40k cost for £10–15k net at one college (dropped for four years); £50k+ at another; a third was the first to stop entirely and expects the rest to follow
- **Email is failing:** auto-filtered to promotions, open rates declining — said independently by nearly every office
- **Brasenose: "Hubbub is expensive; Dono is free for colleges."** Hubbub builds a new site per campaign, is more corporate, and creates more work for development offices — Dono absorbs that workload
- **Colleges copy colleges:** *"having a college onboard will do bits for other colleges to follow"* *(Brasenose)* — your go-to-market in the customer's own words
- **Colleges are barred from advertising undergraduate funding availability** (it would give richer colleges an admissions advantage) *(Magdalen)* — a structural reason students can't find money that only a third party can solve
- **Young alumni are already giving ~£5/month** where they give at all *(Univ)* — the willingness exists at exactly your price point
- **Donors can't pay directly into student bank accounts** — no audit trail, no gift aid; everything must route through a college account and be manually reconciled and disbursed *(Ben, treasury)*. 100 donations to 100 students = 100 manual allocations. **You remove that burden entirely**
- **Design feedback from a development officer:** praised the receipt-style format, the slick design and the clear per-item cost breakdown; called student-facing item-level specificity the key differentiator from existing crowdfunding pages

## Part 5 — TIER 4: Disconfirming evidence (hold for Q&A — do not put on the slide)

Having sought this out is a maturity signal. Volunteering it unprompted is not. Use it only when challenged, and answer directly.

| Challenge you'll get | What you found | How to answer |
|---|---|---|
| "Doesn't the small→major pipeline theory get disputed?" | A Development Fund head rejects it from experience: the first gift a donor makes is usually already their biggest | Concede it's contested, then note the same person said he'd run a small-giving programme "if I had an unlimited budget" — **for engagement**. Engagement is what we sell; the pipeline is a bonus |
| "Will colleges actually adopt?" | One won't (strict finance committee); another says it's "ahead of the curve for us"; a third expects 5 years before it's commonplace | Colleges are not our customer and don't need to adopt. We need *one* to endorse, and we have a named list of the ones flagged as most open |
| "Someone already tried this" | OxReach (Oxford + Hubbub): 6 projects ever, 4 from professors, not renewed. Southampton ran a crowdfunding platform ~7 years ago; it relied too heavily on students, was inconsistent year to year, and Hubbub moved away from it | Both were institution-owned and institution-sold. The Southampton failure mode — student inconsistency — is exactly why we build around **societies**, which have committees and annual handovers, rather than individuals |
| "Why not just use Crowdfunder?" | Asked directly by a development office; also the traffic problem — "we can't email 9,100 alumni every time a campaign launches" | Because the alumni come through the student's own network, not the college's list. We never need their mailing list |
| "Should you work with universities at all?" | Advisor: *"I do not recommend working with universities"* — procurement rivals government; his own initiative took 3 years to approve | Which is exactly why colleges pay us nothing and sign nothing. We need endorsement, not procurement |
| Risks a development office raised | Bias (students who present better raise more); alumni fatigue; reputational risk from one bad campaign; unclear spend oversight | Every one of these has a product answer already built — human pre-publication review, closure statements with purchase evidence, per-campaign caps under consideration |

## Part 6 — Accuracy flags before you present any of this

1. **"We have raised nothing to date" is on slide 18 and may be wrong.** `company/company-context.md` records that **grant money has been transferred into the Starling account** (31 Jul 2026). Grant funding isn't investment, but the claim as worded is unsafe. Change to *"no equity raised, no external investors"* and mention the grant if asked — it reads as competence, not weakness.

2. **The survey n is 26 responses, 23 of them current Oxford students.** `market-research-notes.md` says n=23 throughout. The numerators are all correct; the denominator is "people who answered that question." Safest phrasing: *"twenty-six responses across fourteen colleges."*

3. **The 73% figure is 8 of 11.** Small n. If pressed, say *"eight of the eleven students in our survey who applied for funding"* rather than defending a percentage.

4. **One college explicitly asked not to be named** in any written material. The deck anonymises all institutions by role and scale for consistency. Keep it that way unless you get written consent.

5. **Quotes are compressed paraphrases from meeting notes, not verbatim transcripts.** If asked directly, say so.

6. **The India Society campaign has two open dependencies** in your own TODO: speak to Cathy before officially launching, and resolve Step's concerns about the Somerville demo. **Confirm the campaign is genuinely live before you claim it on stage.** A judge who checks and finds it isn't live costs you more than the slide gains.

7. **Insurance:** `company-context.md` says business insurance was added 31 Jul; `TRUTH.md` of the same date says Dono is an uninsured sole trader. Don't claim insurance until this is reconciled.

## Part 7 — What I'd actually put on the slide

A traction slide holds about nine facts before it stops being read. Recommended selection:

**Four headline numbers**
> **11** development offices interviewed · **26** students surveyed across **14** colleges · **60k+** alumni represented · **1** live campaign

**Three evidence rows**
- **Product is built and live** — Stripe payments, human review, society verification, moderation portal, all shipped
- **The people the system failed are our most likely users** — students rejected for funding score 7.1/10 on likelihood to use Dono, against a 6.4 average
- **A college is waiting on a demo case** — colleges copy colleges; the first one is the only hard one

**Three quotes, ≤8 words each**
- *"Get young alumni onto the ladder."* — Director of Development, 10,000-alumni college
- *"Small donations function as test gifts."* — Head of Donor Relations
- *"Hubbub is expensive. Dono is free."* — Oxford college, pitch meeting

**One killer stat, given its own line**
> **£75,000 from 200 donors. Five years ago: £56,000 from 200 donors.**
> Colleges aren't finding new donors. They're asking the same ones for more.

That last one may deserve to move earlier in the deck — it's arguably a *problem* slide fact rather than a traction fact, and it would strengthen slide 6 (young alumni) considerably.

## Part 8 — The three gaps worth closing before you pitch

1. **Alumni interviews. You have two, both non-donors, and the sample was abandoned.** Alumni are the paying customer. Roger flagged this as make-or-break and he's right — it is the largest hole in the evidence base. Ten conversations would change the slide. Even five would let you say "we asked our customers" instead of "we asked our users."

2. **Behavioural data, not stated intent.** Nobody has been asked *"have you ever used GoFundMe or JustGiving for a university cost, and if not, why not?"* That question converts stated intent into revealed preference and is worth more than another twenty likelihood scores.

3. **Any demand-side proof at all.** Every number you have is supply-side — students who would ask. You have nothing showing alumni will give to a stranger. **The India Society campaign is the fix**: if it raises anything at all before the pitch, that single figure outweighs most of this document. Prioritise getting it live and getting a number on the board.
